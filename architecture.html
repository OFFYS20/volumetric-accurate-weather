Module 01 · Foundation

The Map

One authoritative atmospheric state, six consumers, and a hard rule: no phenomenon owns a pixel. Clouds, dust, ash and plasma all resolve through the same march, differing only in the coefficients they write into the medium.

Design axioms

State, not triggers

Nothing is spawned by a button. A driver polls the solved field, evaluates a physical gate (“is 0–1 km SRH above 150 m²s⁻² while LCL sits under 1200 m?”) and only then injects source terms. Removing the gate removes the phenomenon.

One medium, many coefficients

Cloud droplets, dust, ash and ice all write into a single σs, σa, g, albedo set per voxel. The march never branches on phenomenon type — only on optical depth.

Cost is spatial, not phenomenal

A tornado costs what its bounding brick set costs. Adding the twentieth phenomenon adds drivers on the compute queue, never a new full-screen pass.

Everything must reproject

Any effect that cannot supply a motion vector is disqualified from the quarter-resolution path and must be authored as a full-resolution analytic pass (halos, glories, pillars).

Frame graph

Simulation runs on the async compute queue at a fixed 30 Hz and is double buffered; the graphics queue always reads the last completed state and interpolates. A stalled solver degrades into advection-only drift rather than a hitch.

The phenomenon driver

Every entry in all three tiers implements the same 40-line interface. A driver may read the field, write source terms, push emissive events and own a small particle allocation — it may not draw. This is what keeps the taxonomy honest: a phenomenon is a set of forces plus a gate, never a renderer.

Why hysteresis matters. Raw gates chatter: a field diagnostic crosses its threshold for two ticks and a funnel flickers. Every gate integrates a sustain window before firing and a longer decay window before releasing, which also gives the renderer time to stream in the brick set the driver is about to fill.

Time, scale and determinism

  • Fixed 30 Hz solver, variable render. The renderer lerps between state n−1 and n; velocity is lerped, density is advected forward by the residual time so cloud detail never visibly pops between ticks.
  • Time compression 1×–3600×. Above ~60× the solver switches from per-tick microphysics to a quasi-equilibrium saturation adjustment and the render-time detail noise is frozen in the air-parcel frame, so a day of cloud evolution costs the same as a minute.
  • Deterministic replay. All stochastic terms (turbulence seeding, DBM branch choice, nucleation sites) draw from a counter-based RNG keyed on (tick, cellIndex, streamId). A scenario plus a seed reproduces bit-identically on the same driver.
  • Authoring surface. A scenario is a sounding, a hodograph, a terrain reference, and a list of forcings — see the Physics API.

Module 02 · Tier A

Baseline Mechanics & Optics

The always-on layer. Tier A must hold up at every camera altitude, in every lighting condition, at full frame rate, with no driver active — it is the floor the other two tiers stand on. Six of the seven resolve inside the single cloud march; only the halo leaves it.

Phenomena

Cumuliform layers

raymarch · density threshold

Base shape from a tiling 128³ Perlin–Worley volume remapped by a coverage term, eroded at the boundary by a 32³ Worley volume whose strength ramps with altitude, then multiplied by a height-gradient profile that gives the type its silhouette — humilis is a squashed gradient, congestus a tall one with an eroded cauliflower crown. The threshold itself is not artistic: it is the saturation surface, so the base is flat because every column reaches the LCL at the same height.

Driven by
LCL height (base), w from CAPE (top), qc (opacity), EL (cap)
Detail
Curl-noise advection of the erosion lookup in the parcel frame; no sim cost
Cost
Shares the primary march. 64–128 steps, ~3.2 ms at quarter res

Stratiform decks

raymarch · slab, anisotropic erosion

Same volume, different gradient: a strong capping inversion clamps vertical development, so the height profile collapses to a slab 200–600 m thick and the erosion becomes horizontally anisotropic (billows stretch along the mean wind). Because optical depth saturates quickly, the march detects a slab case from the occupancy pyramid and drops to a short fixed step count — a full overcast is the cheapest sky in the sandbox, not the most expensive.

Driven by
Inversion strength Γinv, relative humidity in the boundary layer, subsidence
Variants
Stratus, stratocumulus (open/closed cell from surface flux), altostratus, cirrostratus
Cost
24–40 steps; early-out at T < 0.01 usually inside 30 m of the deck

Virga

particle advection → extinction volume

Rain mixing ratio qr is generated by autoconversion above and falls at terminal velocity into the subsaturated air below the base, evaporating on the way. The visible shaft is the extinction of that qr field, so the shaft always ends where the air is dry enough to consume the drops — the classic ragged trailing hook is a shear effect on a falling column, not a texture. Full evaporation also cools the layer, which is exactly the seed a microburst driver needs.

Driven by
qr, sub-cloud dew-point depression, fall speed vt(D), 0–3 km shear
Model
Advected at half sim resolution with an anisotropic vertical phase lobe; rendered in the same march with a streak-biased sample kernel
Cost
+0.4 ms sim; no additional render pass

Radiation fog

analytic slab + terrain drainage field

Nocturnal surface cooling drives the surface temperature toward the dew point; where it crosses, a fog layer condenses whose depth is solved on a 2D height field rather than in the 3D volume. Cold air drains downhill, so the field is advected by a shallow-water style gravity flow over the terrain — valleys fill, ridges stay clear, and the top of the layer is dead flat because it is an inversion surface. Rendered as an exponential-in-height medium with depth-aware compositing against opaque geometry.

Driven by
Net longwave loss, wind speed (mixes it out above ~3 m/s), soil moisture, terrain slope
Model
1024² R16F depth + density; 3D lookup is analytic, so fog costs 8 steps regardless of extent
Cost
0.25 ms including the drainage step

Crepuscular rays

in-scatter integration under cloud shadow

Not a screen-space effect. The aerial-perspective froxel grid (32×32×32 over the near 2–16 km) samples the same cloud transmittance map the clouds use to shade themselves, so every froxel knows whether it is lit. Integrating in-scattered sunlight through that grid produces shafts, anticrepuscular rays at the antisolar point, and correct shadowing of the haze by terrain, all for free. Only the near-field air is affected — the rays are the aerosol scattering, so they strengthen with haze and vanish in clean polar air.

Driven by
Aerosol optical depth, boundary-layer humidity, sun elevation, cloud coverage gaps
Model
Froxel in-scatter × cloud shadow map; jittered per-froxel depth slice, TAA-resolved
Cost
0.35 ms (the froxel grid is needed for aerial perspective regardless)

22° ice halos

angular phase LUT · full-res post pass

Halos are a scattering-angle phenomenon, not a density phenomenon: randomly oriented hexagonal prisms have a minimum deviation of 21.8° through their 60° faces and 45.7° through the 90° prism, and the sharp inner edge with the red-inside dispersion falls out of the deviation curve. The sandbox precomputes a 1D LUT over deviation angle per wavelength band and applies it in a full-resolution pass weighted by the ice optical depth accumulated along the view ray. Orienting the crystal population (plate order parameter) migrates energy from the ring into parhelia, the parhelic circle and the upper tangent arc.

Driven by
Cirrus ice water path, crystal habit, orientation order parameter, sun elevation
Model
1024×3 LUT (deviation × RGB); crystal habit selects between four baked LUTs
Cost
0.18 ms — it must run at full resolution or the 0.5° inner edge aliases

Lenticular clouds

standing-wave condensation envelope

The air moves, the cloud does not. Terrain is transformed once into a spectral forcing; the stationary gravity-wave response is evaluated from the Scorer parameter ℓ² = N²/U² − U″/U, giving a vertical displacement field η(x,z) that is fixed in world space. A parcel riding a wave crest cools adiabatically, crosses saturation and condenses; descending on the far side it evaporates. The visible lens is therefore the intersection of the wave crest with the layer where the LCL is exceeded — smooth-edged, stacked when the humidity profile is layered, and completely stationary in a 25 m/s wind.

Driven by
Brunt–Väisälä frequency N, cross-barrier wind U(z), moisture layering, terrain spectrum
Model
2D FFT of terrain → η(x,z) evaluated in the density shader; density = f(η, RH(z))
Cost
0.1 ms amortised (recomputed only when U or N changes materially)

The angular optics stack

Halos, glories and pillars share one machine: a scattering-angle evaluation weighted by the optical depth of a specific particle population. All three run in a single half-resolution pass (the halo channel is upsampled with a sharpening filter to keep the inner edge crisp) after the clouds are composited but before bloom, so they inherit correct occlusion from any cloud in front of them.

Why a LUT and not a live Monte-Carlo. A physically sampled crystal population needs millions of ray/prism intersections to resolve a clean 22° ring. The LUTs are baked offline from that exact simulation — one bake per habit (column, plate, bullet rosette, Parry-oriented) — so the runtime keeps the physics and pays 0.18 ms.

Module 03 · Tier B

Severe & Destructive Forces

Tier B is where the sandbox earns its name. Each of these is a driver that adds force and heat to the field and then gets out of the way — the anvil spreads because the parcel ran out of buoyancy, the funnel narrows because the pressure deficit deepened, the outflow curls because a vortex ring formed when the downdraft hit the ground.

Phenomena

Supercells with anvils

buoyant plume in a sheared field · nested cascade

No supercell object exists. A moist boundary-layer bubble is lifted past its LFC, latent heat release accelerates it, and the 0–6 km shear tilts the updraft off the vertical so precipitation falls beside rather than into the inflow — which is the entire reason the storm becomes steady-state and long-lived. At the equilibrium level the parcel is neutrally buoyant and momentum alone carries it into the stable stratosphere, producing the overshooting top; the outflow spreads laterally into the anvil and shears downwind. The 50 m nested grid follows the updraft core automatically.

Driven by
CAPE, CIN, LCL, 0–6 km bulk shear, BRN, EL and tropopause height
Emergent features
Overshooting top, backsheared anvil, mammatus on the anvil underside, wall cloud, flanking line, striated mesocyclone banding
Cost
+1.1 ms (nested L2 grid) and roughly 2× march steps inside the storm bricks

Tornadic funnels & vortices

analytic vortex line + condensation SDF + debris particles

A hybrid, because a 50 m grid can never resolve a 30 m core. A spline of vortex nodes carries circulation Γ and core radius rc; it is advected and tilted by the resolved field, while injecting its own induced velocity back into the grid so the storm feels it. The visible funnel is not modelled art: cyclostrophic balance gives the pressure deficit, the deficit lowers the saturation temperature, and the condensation surface is the radius where the local air reaches saturation — so the funnel is fat and smooth in a moist low-LCL environment, and a thin rope with only a debris cloud when the LCL is high. Multi-vortex structure appears when the swirl ratio exceeds ~0.8, as sub-vortices spawned along the parent core.

Driven by
SRH0–1, stretching term (ω·∇)u, LCL height, swirl ratio S, surface roughness
Debris
2M GPU particles, indirect dispatch, advected by field + sub-grid turbulence; splatted back into the medium at half res so they self-shadow coherently
Cost
0.9 ms particles + 0.2 ms vortex solve

Microburst outflows

negatively buoyant parcel → toroidal vortex ring

Evaporating precipitation cools a column below the cloud; the negatively buoyant air accelerates downward, impinges on the surface and turns radially outward. The signature curl is a genuine vortex ring formed at the stagnation point — it is generated by the no-slip boundary condition, not authored. Dry microbursts show almost no cloud and a violent dust ring; wet ones show a rain foot that flares outward at the ground. Divergent outflow registers as a strong negative in the diagnostic field, which is what the sandbox's aviation-hazard overlay reads.

Driven by
Sub-cloud lapse rate (DCAPE), precipitation loading, mid-level dryness
Model
Source term −g·(θ′/θ₀ + qr) with a ring-vorticity generator at the surface interface
Cost
Free — it is the solver plus a boundary condition

Haboobs

gravity-current front · Rouse suspension profile

The outflow above, given a desert to run over. A density-current front propagates at U = k√(g′h) with k ≈ 0.9; behind it, surface stress exceeding the threshold friction velocity lofts dust whose vertical concentration profile follows the Rouse number P = ws/κu*. The wall is a wall because a gravity current has a raised head and a billowing Kelvin–Helmholtz roll behind it — the same shear instability described in the physics module, at 500 m scale. Dust is a high-albedo, strongly forward-scattering medium with a spectral tint, so a haboob glows orange from the sunward side and is nearly black from within.

Driven by
Outflow buoyancy g′, surface friction velocity u*, particle size, soil availability
Model
2D front solver on the terrain feeding 3D aerosol concentration; roll vortices resolved on the L2 grid
Cost
0.3 ms front + shared march

Volcanic ash plumes with internal lightning

MTT buoyant plume + tribo-electric DBM discharge

Three regimes in one column, from the classical plume solution: a momentum-driven gas thrust jet, a buoyancy-driven convective region entraining ambient air, and an umbrella cloud spreading at the neutral buoyancy level (H ≈ 8.2·Q̇¼ for heat flux in MW). Ash is optically the opposite of cloud — high extinction, low single-scatter albedo — so it reads as a solid silhouette and needs almost no multi-scattering octaves. Charge separates by tribo-electrification wherever the ash concentration gradient and turbulent shear are both high; when the local field exceeds breakdown, a dielectric-breakdown-model tree grows through the charged region and is injected as a two-frame emissive plus a real light in the in-scatter list, so the whole plume flickers from inside.

Driven by
Mass eruption rate, vent radius, exit velocity, ash grain size distribution, ambient stratification
Emergent
Column collapse into a pyroclastic density current when the eruption is too dense to entrain enough air
Cost
0.4 ms plume + 0.15 ms discharge tree

Fujiwhara merging

mutual vortex advection on the synoptic grid

Two cyclonic circulations on the L0 grid each move in the other's induced flow (V ≈ Γ/2πd), so they orbit their circulation-weighted centroid — no orbit code, just advection. When separation falls below roughly 1.5 core radii the vorticity fields shear into filaments and axisymmetrise into a single larger, weaker-then-reintensifying vortex. Everything visible — the spiral banding, the merging eyewalls, the strained-out filaments — is the cloud field being deformed by that flow. Also runs for mesoscale vortices, which is how storm splitting and cell mergers happen in Tier B.

Driven by
Circulation Γ1,2, separation d, core radii, β-drift, environmental steering flow
Model
Vortices are diagnostics on the coarse field, not objects; they are detected each tick by an Okubo–Weiss / vorticity-centroid pass
Cost
<0.05 ms (L0 is 128×128×48 at 1 Hz)

Vortex line & condensation funnel

The one place where a sub-grid analytic model is unavoidable. The vortex is a Burgers–Rott profile — a Rankine core with viscous smoothing and axial stretching — sampled directly in the density shader. Its radius comes out of the thermodynamics, which is why raising the dew point visibly fattens the funnel without touching a single art parameter.

Discharge model — storm and ash lightning

One implementation serves cloud-to-ground strokes, intracloud spider lightning and volcanic plume discharges, because they differ only in the charge structure that feeds them.

  • Charging. In convective storms, non-inductive graupel–ice collisions in the mixed-phase zone (−10 to −25 °C) build the classic tripole. In ash plumes, tribo-electrification is driven by the product of concentration gradient and turbulent dissipation. Both write to the same scalar charge field ρq.
  • Initiation. A Poisson solve on ρq gives the potential; when |E| exceeds the altitude-scaled breakdown threshold anywhere, a leader is seeded there.
  • Propagation. Dielectric breakdown model: growth probability at a candidate cell ∝ (φ − φc)η with η ≈ 2. This gives the branching statistics for free — raise η and the channel straightens toward a rocket-triggered look, lower it for spidery intracloud webs.
  • Rendering. Channels are emissive line segments injected into the medium for 2–4 frames with a decaying afterglow, plus up to 32 point lights pushed into the in-scatter light list so the illumination is genuinely volumetric — the flash lights the inside of the cloud, not a screen-space flash.
  • Charge relaxation. Each discharge subtracts the neutralised charge from ρq, which is what sets the natural flash rate. Flash rate is an output of the storm, not an input.

Module 04 · Tier C

Rare & Upper-Atmospheric

Phenomena that most people never see, which is precisely the point of a sandbox. Two rules keep them from becoming toys: each has a physically stated occurrence window that the world state must satisfy, and none of them may cost anything when its window is closed.

Phenomena

Red sprites & blue jets

emissive streamer volume · additive, unlit

Transient luminous events are not clouds and must not be rendered like them: they emit, they do not scatter meaningfully, and they last 3–100 ms. When a positive cloud-to-ground stroke removes a large charge moment (ΔMq above roughly 300–600 C·km), the quasi-electrostatic field above the storm exceeds the conventional breakdown threshold at the altitude where E/N is largest — near 75 km. A streamer tree grows downward from there with the same DBM kernel Tier B uses for lightning, but with altitude-dependent branching: tenuous air gives long, thin, carrot-shaped columns; denser air below 55 km gives the tendril fringe. Colour is emission-band physics, not art direction — N₂ first positive (650–750 nm) for the red body, N₂⁺ first negative (427.8 nm) for blue jets and the streamer tips.

Driven by
Parent stroke polarity and charge moment change, mesospheric density profile, storm top height
Render path
Separate 32-step emissive march on the mesospheric shell, additive, no shadowing, resolved before atmospheric transmittance is applied
Cost
0.25 ms while active, zero otherwise; typically 1 event per 200 negative strokes

Noctilucent & nacreous clouds

curved shell + Mie interference LUT

Two different clouds sharing one render path: a thin sheet of ice lit from below the horizon. Noctilucent clouds sit at 76–85 km in the polar summer mesopause, made of ~50 nm ice; they are visible only while the sun is 6°–16° below the horizon, because higher and the sky outshines them, lower and the sheet itself is unlit. Their particles are small compared to visible wavelengths, so scattering is strongly forward and blue-shifted — that electric blue is Mie theory, and the billow pattern is a gravity-wave signature propagating up from the troposphere. Nacreous clouds are stratospheric (15–25 km) lee-wave clouds that need T below about −78 °C; their iridescence comes from a very narrow droplet size distribution, so the sandbox indexes a Mie interference LUT by scattering angle and size parameter to get the mother-of-pearl banding rather than tinting a texture.

Driven by
Sun elevation (−6° to −16°), latitude and season, mesopause temperature, stratospheric wave activity
Model
Analytic shell intersection with planet curvature (mandatory at 82 km — the layer is below the horizon for a ground observer), density from an advected 2D wave field
Cost
0.08 ms — a single ray/shell intersection and one texture fetch

Ball lightning

emissive agent · in-scatter light injection

The one phenomenon with no accepted physical mechanism, so the sandbox is explicit about modelling the observations rather than a theory: a 10–40 cm luminous sphere, lifetime 1–10 s, drifting near-horizontally at walking pace, occasionally attaching to conductors, ending in either a silent fade or a sharp report. Implemented as a small emissive agent — a plasma-noise shell around a bright core — advected by local wind plus a biased random walk, pushed into the same light list volcanic lightning uses so it genuinely illuminates fog, dust and rain around it. Three literature-derived behaviour presets (silicon-vapour oxidation, standing microwave discharge, surface-charge) select drift and termination statistics.

Driven by
Recent CG stroke within 200 m, surface conductivity, preset choice
Model
Agent + point light; the shell is signed-distance raymarched at 12 steps in the same pass as the clouds
Cost
0.05 ms per instance

Fallstreak holes (cavum)

2D phase field · Bergeron–Findeisen depletion

A supercooled altocumulus deck is metastable: liquid droplets at −5 to −20 °C that have simply never found an ice nucleus. An aircraft climbing through it cools the air 20–30 K by adiabatic expansion over the wing or propeller tips, crossing the homogeneous nucleation threshold near −38 °C and seeding ice. Ice then grows at the expense of the liquid, because saturation vapour pressure over ice is lower than over water — the Wegener–Bergeron–Findeisen process. The crystals grow heavy, fall out as virga, and leave a hole that expands radially at 1–4 m/s, stretched into a canal by the shear. Modelled as a two-channel 2D field (liquid fraction, ice fraction) on the deck plus the standard virga volume beneath.

Driven by
Deck temperature (must be between −5 and −38 °C), liquid water path, ice nucleus scarcity, aircraft track
Emergent
Circular holes from a climbing aircraft, long canals from a level pass, iridescence on the fresh ice fringe
Cost
0.06 ms

Glories & Brocken spectres

backscatter LUT + shadow projection at the antisolar point

A glory is a backscattering resonance from droplets comparable in size to the wavelength: concentric coloured rings centred exactly on the antisolar point — that is, on the shadow of the observer's own head. Ring angular radius scales as λ/reff, so a fresh cloud of 5 µm droplets gives wide rings and a drizzling 25 µm deck gives tight ones; the sandbox reads reff from the population channel written during the march, so the rings shrink correctly as a cloud ages. The Brocken spectre is the companion effect and needs no new machinery: it is the observer's silhouette cast into the fog, rendered by projecting the camera's own shadow through the participating medium, apparently gigantic because the eye misjudges distance in a formless fog bank.

Driven by
Antisolar point within the fog/cloud footprint, camera above the deck, droplet reff and distribution width
Model
Shares the angular optics pass (Tier A); the spectre uses the standard shadow cascade with the observer proxy enabled
Cost
Included in the 0.18 ms optics pass

Light pillars

specular smear off oriented plate crystals

Not a beam of light — a stack of reflections. Plate crystals in diamond dust fall with their faces horizontal, wobbling by a tilt spread σt of one to three degrees. Each plate acts as a tiny horizontal mirror, so the observer sees the reflection of the light source in a vertical line of crystals, extending upward roughly in proportion to σt and to how high the crystals are. Every light in the world can produce one: the sun near the horizon, the moon, and — the reason a winter town looks alien — every street lamp individually. Implemented as a vertical anisotropic lobe in the angular optics pass, with a per-light screen-space extent, gated by the diamond-dust population channel.

Driven by
Diamond-dust concentration, tilt spread σt, surface temperature below ~−10 °C, light list
Model
Analytic line integral per light (see the Tier A snippet); up to 64 pillar lights per frame, sorted by screen-space energy
Cost
Included in the 0.18 ms optics pass

The upper-atmosphere render path

Tropospheric machinery breaks above about 20 km. Three changes make Tier C tractable:

  • Spherical shells, not slabs. Every layer above the troposphere is intersected as a ray/sphere pair against the planet radius. At 82 km the geometric horizon is roughly 1000 km away and the layer curves visibly across the sky — a flat slab produces a conspicuously wrong perspective and cannot show the terminator lighting that defines a noctilucent display.
  • Emission before transmittance. Sprites and jets are radiance sources at 40–90 km; they are accumulated in their own march, then attenuated by the atmospheric transmittance LUT for the observer's altitude. Compositing them after aerial perspective — the usual shortcut — makes them look like decals stuck on the sky.
  • Occurrence windows instead of density. These layers have almost no optical depth, so the march never finds them by sampling. Each is evaluated analytically and gated by its window: if the sun is 3° below the horizon, the noctilucent path is not merely dark, it is skipped entirely.

Module 05 · Simulation

Atmospheric Physics & API

The sandbox is authored the way a forecaster reads the atmosphere: a vertical sounding and a hodograph. Everything visible — cloud base, tower height, storm mode, rotation, whether a funnel reaches the ground — is derived from those two curves and the terrain beneath them. There is no “make a supercell” button, and there does not need to be one.

Grid cascade

Four levels, each solving what it can afford to resolve, coupled one-way downscale and two-way for momentum in the storm nest. Level 3 is not simulated at all: it is procedural detail evaluated in the density shader, advected in the parcel frame so it moves with the air instead of swimming through it.

The user edits a sounding — temperature and dew point against pressure — on a skew-T log-p diagram, exactly the instrument the physics is written in. The parcel curve, the level annotations and the shaded energy areas are computed live from the edit, so the relationship between “I dragged the dew point up 3 K” and “the cloud base dropped 375 m” is visible before anything renders.

Wind shear & vorticity

The velocity field is a sum of three terms with three different update rates, which is what makes a 1024 km domain affordable:

  • Advection. Semi-Lagrangian with a MacCormack/BFECC correction: back-trace, forward-trace, and subtract the round-trip error, restoring second-order accuracy for one extra trace. Clamped to the local min/max of the source stencil so the correction cannot overshoot into negative mixing ratios.
  • Incompressibility. The atmosphere is stratified, so the sandbox uses the anelastic constraint ∇·(ρ₀u) = 0 rather than plain ∇·u = 0 — otherwise a parcel rising 10 km never expands and updrafts come out far too narrow. Solved by a red-black multigrid V-cycle, three cycles per tick, which beats 40+ Jacobi iterations for the same residual.
  • Vorticity. ζ = ∇×u is diagnosed, not integrated, but the terms of its evolution are what the drivers read:

A supercell's rotation is the first term doing two jobs in sequence. Vertical shear puts horizontal vorticity in the environment — think of a rolling log lying across the wind. An updraft tilts that log upright, giving vertical vorticity through the mid-levels: the mesocyclone. Then the updraft's own stretching (∂w/∂z > 0) concentrates it, spinning it up the way a skater pulls their arms in. Whether the hodograph is straight or curved decides what happens next: straight shear produces mirror-image left and right movers that split apart; curved shear favours one of them, which is why real supercells are usually right-movers in the northern hemisphere. None of this is coded — it falls out of the term, given the hodograph.

Kelvin–Helmholtz instability

Billow clouds are a shear instability, and the honest way to get them is to let the shear layer go unstable. The obstacle is resolution: the fastest-growing mode has a wavelength of roughly 7.5 times the shear-layer depth, and a 200 m-deep layer therefore wants 1.5 km billows that an L1 cell can barely see. The sandbox meets it halfway.

  • Detect. Compute the gradient Richardson number Ri = N²/(∂u/∂z)² on every column. Ri < 0.25 marks a layer as unstable; the layer's depth h and shear ∂u/∂z come with it.
  • Seed. Rather than waiting for round-off to trigger the instability, inject a small divergence-free perturbation at the analytic fastest-growing wavelength λ ≈ 7.5·h, with amplitude proportional to the growth rate σ ≈ 0.19·(∂u/∂z). This is not faking the billows — it is giving the correct mode the initial condition that a real atmosphere gets from noise.
  • Let it run. The roll-up, the cat's-eye pattern, the pairing of neighbouring billows and the eventual collapse into turbulence are all solved. Because the cloud is drawn from saturation, billows are visible only where the layer is near enough to saturation to condense on the crests — which is exactly when you see them in the sky.
  • Reuse. The same mechanism drives the roll vortices on a haboob head, the shear instability on the top of a gust front, and the striations on a mesocyclone's flank.

Authoring API

A scenario is data. It names an environment, not an outcome — which means the same file can produce a lonely cumulus field or an outbreak depending on the terrain and the time of day you point it at.

The contract of encourage(). It is the only concession to direct authoring, and it deliberately does not spawn anything. It searches the parameter space for the smallest edit to the environment that satisfies the target gate, applies it over a blend window, and hands back a diff you can inspect — so the sandbox stays a simulator even when it is being used as a toy.

Raymarch Pipeline

One march resolves clouds, dust, ash, rain and fog. It runs at a quarter of the pixels and a sixteenth of the samples a naive implementation would need, and the machinery that makes that survivable — occupancy skipping, blue-noise offsets, cloud-space motion vectors, variance-clamped history — is the actual engineering content of this module.

Sampling strategy

Three techniques, applied in this order, cut the sample count by roughly 8× against a fixed-step march of the same visual quality.

  • Empty-space skipping via an occupancy pyramid. The bake writes a min/max σt per 8³-voxel brick and reduces it into four mips. The ray runs a 3D DDA over that pyramid: at an empty brick it advances to the brick exit at the coarsest mip that is still empty, so an entirely clear sky costs about 20 traversal steps and no density samples at all.
  • Two-tier density sampling. Inside an occupied brick the ray first samples the cheap low-frequency density (three texture fetches). Only when that is positive does it step back one interval and switch to the expensive detailed sample with erosion noise and curl advection. After a run of empty detailed samples it returns to cheap mode. Roughly 70% of samples inside clouds never touch the detail noise.
  • Distance-proportional step size. Step length grows with the ray-cone footprint, dt = clamp(t · 0.012, dtMin, dtMax), so distant clouds are sampled at their projected resolution rather than at their world resolution. A cloud on the horizon 80 km away costs the same as one 4 km away.
  • Spatiotemporal blue noise offset. The first step is offset by a blue-noise value animated by the golden ratio over frames. This converts banding into high-frequency noise that is spatially uncorrelated — precisely the error distribution the temporal resolve removes best. It is a prerequisite for the whole quarter-resolution scheme, not a polish item.

The march loop

Scattering & phase

Clouds are bright and soft because light bounces inside them tens of times before it leaves. Simulating that path-traced is out of budget, so the sandbox uses three approximations that each buy back a specific piece of the appearance.

Beer–Powder

Beer's law alone makes cloud edges too dark, because a thin edge attenuates little but also scatters little back. The powder term adds the missing forward-scattering darkening — 1 − exp(−2σd) — that makes a backlit cumulus read as fluffy rather than as fog.

Multi-scatter octaves

Three octaves, each with attenuated extinction, contribution and phase eccentricity (a=b=c=0.5). Octave 0 is the direct lobe; octaves 1–2 approximate the wide, nearly isotropic glow of deep multiple scattering that lights the shaded side.

Dual-lobe phase

Water droplets scatter hard forward and mildly back. A blend of two Henyey–Greenstein lobes (g = 0.8 / −0.2) gives both the silver lining and the backscatter brightening; a Draine-style term recovers the sharper forward peak for the sun disc region.

Ambient probe

Sky light is applied as a vertical gradient attenuated by depth below the cloud top, which is a two-line approximation of ambient occlusion inside a participating medium and costs nothing.

Temporal reconstruction

The primary march runs at 960×540 for a 3840×2160 output — one marched sample per sixteen display pixels. Each frame marches a different pixel of a 4×4 Bayer cell, so a static camera has a fully converged image after sixteen frames (a quarter of a second), and a moving one degrades gracefully rather than falling apart.

Module 07 · Performance

Budget & Scaling

Measured on an RTX 4070 at 3840×2160, sixty frames per second, with a mature supercell filling a third of the screen — the worst case that is worth optimising for, since a clear sky costs a fifth of this and a full overcast costs less than a broken cumulus field.

Frame budget

Fig. 5 — The march is half the frame. Everything else exists to keep it that small.

  • Graphics queue: 7.45 ms. The primary march at 3.30 ms is 44% of it, which is the right shape — if the support passes ever exceed the march, the optimisation effort is in the wrong place.
  • Async queue: 2.28 ms per 30 Hz tick = 1.14 ms per rendered frame. In practice the scheduler hides 0.2–0.9 ms of it inside graphics-queue bubbles, so wall-clock frame time lands between 7.7 and 8.6 ms against a 16.6 ms budget.
  • Headroom is deliberate. The sandbox is a component, not an application: half the frame is left for whatever it is embedded in. Standalone, the same scene runs at 120 Hz.
  • Clear sky: 1.6 ms. Empty-space skipping means the march exits after ~20 traversal steps. Full overcast: 4.1 ms, because transmittance collapses inside the first 200 m of the deck and the loop breaks early.

Adaptive controller

The march reports its own step count. A proportional controller compares the measured march time against its slice of the budget and adjusts three knobs between frames, in this order:

  • Step-size multiplier (1.0 → 1.6). Cheapest, least visible; recovers about 30% of march cost before detail loss becomes noticeable.
  • Max step count (128 → 64). Truncates the deepest rays first, which affects the interiors of large storms — visible mainly as slightly flatter internal shading.
  • Re-march list cap (4% → 0% of pixels). Trades disocclusion recovery speed for time; the first thing to go when the camera is moving fast, because motion masks it anyway.
  • Never touched automatically: resolution, octave count, sim rate. Resolution changes are visible as a pop, and dropping the sim rate changes the weather, which is a correctness bug, not a performance measure.
